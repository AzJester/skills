# Structured interviewing

Unstructured interviews predict job performance barely better than chance, and interviewers are confident in them anyway. That combination — low validity, high confidence — is why structure matters.

Structure means: the same questions, in the same order, against criteria defined before anyone was seen, scored independently before discussion. Every departure from that reduces the signal, and every departure feels reasonable at the time.

## Step 1: Define what you are hiring for, before writing questions

Not a job description. A short list of what actually determines success in this role, on this team, now.

For each attribute, state what evidence would demonstrate it. If you cannot describe the evidence, you cannot interview for it, and "culture fit" is where this fails hardest — it either means something specific and describable, or it means "people like us", which is a bias with a friendly name.

Four to six attributes. More produces a loop where everything is assessed shallowly.

Distinguish **must-have** from **would-strengthen**, and from **can be learned here**. Treating a learnable skill as a requirement is how strong candidates get filtered out.

## Step 2: Build the scorecard first

Write it before the questions, and certainly before meeting anyone.

| Attribute | What strong looks like | What weak looks like | Which interview assesses it | Evidence collected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

**What strong looks like** is the hard column and the one that makes the scorecard work. Behavioral anchors — a described observable, not "excellent communication".

**Two interviewers per attribute** where it matters. One assessment is one person's read.

## Step 3: Write questions that produce evidence

**Behavioral** — past behavior, a strong predictor of future behavior: *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision your team had already made. What did you do?"*

Probe for specifics. A candidate describing what they *would* do is giving you a theory; press for what they *did*. "What was your specific contribution?" separates the person from the team.

**Work sample** — the strongest signal available. A realistic task, done under realistic conditions: review this design and tell us what worries you; here is a failing system, diagnose it; write the memo recommending one of these options.

Sample the actual work. Puzzle questions and algorithm trivia measure preparation for puzzle questions and algorithm trivia.

**Situational** — for scenarios the candidate is unlikely to have faced: *"You discover two weeks before delivery that a subcontractor's component fails an acceptance test. Walk me through the next 48 hours."*

Avoid: leading questions, questions with an obvious right answer, anything a well-prepared candidate answers identically regardless of ability, and anything touching protected characteristics or subjects unrelated to the work.

## Step 4: Run it consistently

- **Same questions, same order**, every candidate. Comparison requires it.
- **Take notes during, not after.** Memory reconstructs toward the impression formed in the first minutes.
- **Record what they said**, separately from what you concluded. Conclusions recorded without evidence cannot be reviewed by anyone else.
- **Interviewers prepare.** An interviewer reading the résumé in the first two minutes wastes the slot and signals the organization's seriousness to the candidate.
- **Leave time for their questions.** What a candidate asks is signal, and the interview is also them evaluating you.

## Step 5: Score independently, then debrief

The single highest-leverage rule in the whole process: **every interviewer submits their score and evidence before seeing anyone else's.**

Without it the first person to speak sets the anchor and the debrief becomes consensus-formation rather than evidence-pooling. With it, disagreement surfaces — and disagreement is the most informative thing in the process, because it means different evidence exists.

In the debrief:

- Each interviewer presents **evidence**, not verdicts. "They said X, which showed Y."
- Work the disagreements rather than averaging them. Two people who saw the same candidate differently usually asked different questions or heard different things, and finding out which is the point.
- Decide against the scorecard, not against the room's mood.
- **Missing evidence is a legitimate outcome.** "We did not assess this" means adding a conversation, not guessing.

## Step 6: The biases that survive good process

- **First impressions.** Assessments made in the first minutes, rationalized for the remaining fifty. Structure and note-taking blunt it.
- **Similarity.** Warmth toward candidates who resemble us in background, school, or manner. This is the one that quietly shapes a team.
- **Halo.** One strong attribute inflating everything else. Independent per-attribute scoring blunts it.
- **Anchoring on the first candidate.** Whoever is seen first becomes the reference point.
- **Confidence read as competence.** They correlate weakly. Evidence, not delivery.

You cannot debias people by asking them to be unbiased. The process is the control: fixed questions, defined anchors, independent scoring, evidence-based debrief.

## Calibration

Interviewers drift. Periodically: run two interviewers on the same interview and compare scores, review past decisions against how those hires actually performed, and retire questions that never discriminate between strong and weak candidates.

A question everyone answers well is not assessing anything, and it is occupying a slot that could.
