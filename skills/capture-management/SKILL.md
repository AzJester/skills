# Capture management

`solution-shaping` decides what to offer and notes that the capture-side work lives elsewhere. `engineering-to-proposal` states it "does not decide bid or no-bid." This is that work: everything between noticing an opportunity and the RFP dropping.

**Proposals are won or lost before the RFP is released.** By the time a solicitation is public, the customer usually has a preferred approach, often a preferred vendor, and always a set of expectations formed by whoever was talking to them for the previous year. A proposal is where that position is documented, not where it is created.

The failure this exists to prevent is treating capture as the waiting period before real work starts.

## Step 1: Qualify honestly, and early

Most organizations bid too much. Every pursuit consumes bid and proposal money and the attention of the people who would otherwise be improving the pursuits worth winning.

Five questions, answered with evidence rather than optimism:

| Question | A weak answer sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Is this real?** Funded, in the budget, with a decided acquisition strategy | "It's in their five-year plan" |
| **Do we understand the customer's problem?** Better than the incumbent | "We've read the RFI" |
| **Can we win?** Honest probability, with the incumbent's position priced in | "We're as good as anyone" |
| **Do we want it?** Strategic fit, margin, and what it displaces | "It's revenue" |
| **Can we perform?** Capacity, cleared staff, past performance that qualifies | "We'll hire" |

**Score it and record the score.** A pWin assessment written down and revisited is a management instrument; one carried in someone's head is a mood. Re-score at every gate, and let it fall when the evidence says so.

**A pursuit with no customer contact is not a pursuit.** If nobody in the organization has spoken to the customer about this requirement, the honest pWin is low regardless of technical fit — someone else has been having those conversations.

## Step 2: Gather intelligence, systematically

Capture intelligence is a research task, not networking. What you need:

**The requirement.** What problem is being solved, who is driving it, what has already been tried, and what constraints are non-negotiable. Sources: sources sought notices, RFIs, industry days, draft solicitations, published budget justification documents, prior contract records, conference presentations.

**The people.** Who writes the requirement, who evaluates, who decides, who uses the result. These are rarely the same person, and they frequently want different things.

**The money.** How much, from which appropriation, in which year, and what happens to it if the award slips.

**The incumbent, if there is one.** How they are performing, where they are vulnerable, and what a change of contractor would cost the customer. Incumbency is worth a great deal, and pretending otherwise produces bids priced as if it were not.

**The competition.** Who else will bid, what they will offer, and what they will price. See Step 4.

**Record it in a capture plan that is actually maintained.** The artifact matters less than the discipline of writing down what is known, what is assumed, and what is still unknown — the last category being the one that drives the call plan.

## Step 3: Shape the requirement, legitimately

Pre-solicitation is when the customer's thinking is still forming, and when a contractor can help them see the problem more clearly.

- **Respond substantively to RFIs and sources sought.** They are read, and they inform the acquisition strategy. A response that is a capability brochure wastes the opportunity.
- **Comment on the draft RFP.** Ambiguities, unrealistic schedules and requirements that only one vendor can meet are all worth raising.
- **Offer information the customer lacks**, including where their stated approach carries risk. This builds the relationship that a year of capability briefings does not.

**Stay on the right side of the line.** Shaping means helping a customer understand the problem and the realistic solution space. It does not mean obtaining non-public procurement information, drafting the requirement for them in a way that excludes competitors improperly, or anything that creates an organizational conflict of interest. OCI in particular can disqualify you from the very competition you helped shape — check it early, and involve contracts and counsel when the answer is not obvious. See `teaming-and-subcontracts`.

## Step 4: Assess the competition adversarially

**Run a black hat.** Assign people to bid the opportunity as each likely competitor would: their solution, their discriminators, their price, their team. Done properly it changes your own strategy; done as a slide of competitor logos it changes nothing.

The output is specific: for each likely bidder, what they will propose, what they will claim, where they are strong and where they are exposed, and what they will price. Then the question that matters — **what do we do that they cannot credibly claim?**

Feed this straight into `solution-shaping`, which tests discriminators against exactly this.

## Step 5: Price to win

**Price-to-win is an estimate of what it takes to win, not what it costs you.** They are different numbers and the gap between them is the decision.

- Build it from the customer's budget, the competition's likely cost structure, and the evaluation approach — LPTA and best-value trade-off produce very different targets. See `proposal-writing`.
- Compare it against a real estimate from `cost-estimating-and-boe`, not a wish.
- **Where the estimate exceeds the price-to-win, that is a decision, not an arithmetic problem.** Reduce scope, change the approach, accept lower fee, or no-bid — each made knowingly. Shaving hours quietly until it fits is how a program is won and then executed at a loss.

## Step 6: Gate reviews and the bid decision

Whatever the gates are called, each answers one question and each is an opportunity to stop:

| Gate | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Qualification | Is this worth spending pursuit money on? |
| Pursuit | Do we understand the customer well enough to shape? |
| Bid decision | Do we bid, with what team, at what price? |
| Proposal readiness | Is the solution and the evidence ready to write from? |
| Submission | Is it compliant and does it reflect the strategy? |

**No-bid is a legitimate and under-used outcome.** The cost of a bad bid is not just the B&P money; it is the pursuit that got less attention, and occasionally winning something you cannot perform. A capture process that never no-bids is not a process.

Use `executive-decision-memo` to get the bid decision made — the ask, why now, the options, the recommendation, and what it costs.

## Step 7: Hand off

When the decision is bid, capture does not stop. It supplies:

- The customer's real problem and priorities → `solution-shaping`
- The competitive assessment and discriminators → `solution-shaping`, then `proposal-writing`
- The price-to-win and the descope ladder → `cost-estimating-and-boe`
- The teaming arrangements → `teaming-and-subcontracts`
- The past-performance strategy → `engineering-to-proposal`
- The pursuit and delivery risks → `risk-management`

A proposal team that receives only the RFP is starting a year late.

## Common failures

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capture starts at RFP release | Writing to a requirement shaped by someone else | Engage while the requirement is forming |
| Bidding everything | B&P spread thin, low win rate | Qualify with evidence; no-bid is an outcome |
| pWin as a feeling | Never falls, never stops a pursuit | Score it, record it, re-score at each gate |
| Black hat as a logo slide | Strategy unchanged by it | Assign people to actually bid as the competitor |
| Incumbency discounted | Priced as a level field | Price the switching cost the customer faces |
| Price-to-win as a target to hit | Estimate quietly shaved to fit | Treat the gap as a decision for a person |
| Capture knowledge not handed over | Proposal team rediscovers it late | Structured handoff at the bid decision |

The honest one is the first. Everything downstream — `solution-shaping`, `proposal-writing`, the whole compliance machine — is executing a position that was set months earlier by whoever was in the room.
