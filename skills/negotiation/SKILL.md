# Negotiation

`contract-vehicles-and-clauses` covers what a contract commits you to and says plainly that it does not cover negotiating one. This does.

Two things distinguish negotiation in this environment from the general case, and both change the tactics:

**You will work with these people for years.** A prime, a subcontractor, a program office, a functional peer. Winning a negotiation and damaging the relationship is usually a bad trade, because the relationship is where the next five decisions get made — many of them informally, in your favor or not.

**Much of the hardest negotiation is internal.** Resources, priorities, scope between business units, who absorbs a cost. It has no contract at the end and the same dynamics throughout.

**This is not legal advice.** Contract terms, authority to bind, and anything approaching a dispute belong with contracts and counsel. What follows is preparation and conduct.

## Step 1: Prepare, because preparation is most of the outcome

The negotiation is largely decided before anyone sits down. Establish, in writing:

**Your interests, not your positions.** A position is what you are asking for; an interest is why. "We need twelve weeks" is a position. "We need enough time for qualification testing after the design freeze" is an interest — and it opens options the position closes, like starting qualification earlier or reducing the scope that needs qualifying.

**Their interests, inferred honestly.** What is the person across the table measured on? A program officer facing an obligation deadline, a prime protecting a margin, a functional manager protecting their people — each wants something different from what they are saying.

**Your walk-away, decided in advance.** What you do if there is no agreement, and the point at which no deal beats this deal. Deciding it in the room, under pressure, having already invested effort, produces a worse answer every time.

**Their walk-away, estimated.** What happens to them if this fails? Someone with no alternative negotiates differently from someone with three. This is the single most useful thing to know and the most often not thought about.

**The zone where agreement is possible.** Where your limit and theirs overlap, if they do. Where they do not overlap, the honest conclusion is that there is no deal at this scope — and the productive move is to change the problem rather than to keep meeting.

**Everything that is actually on the table.** Price, scope, schedule, payment terms, data rights, key personnel, options, deliverable frequency, acceptance criteria, warranty. A negotiation framed on one variable is a fight; framed on six it is a trade.

## Step 2: Know your authority and theirs

- **Know exactly what you can commit to** before the conversation, and say when something is above your authority rather than improvising.
- **Find out who can actually decide.** Negotiating with someone without authority is a rehearsal in which you disclose your position and receive nothing.
- **In federal contracting, only the contracting officer can change the contract.** A technical representative agreeing to something is not a modification, and work performed on informal direction may be unrecoverable — `contract-vehicles-and-clauses` covers constructive change and it is the expensive trap here.
- **Where you are the one without authority**, say so early. It costs nothing and it prevents the other side feeling misled later.

## Step 3: Trade rather than concede

**Never give something without getting something.** Not out of gamesmanship — a unilateral concession teaches the other side that pressure works, and invites more of it. "I can do that if you can move the delivery date" is a trade; "fine, we'll absorb it" is a lesson.

**Bundle issues, do not sequence them.** Settling one variable at a time destroys value: each is settled without the flexibility the others would have provided. Put several on the table and trade across them, since what is cheap for you is often expensive for them and vice versa — that asymmetry is where the value in a negotiation actually is.

**Make concessions smaller as you go.** A pattern of shrinking concessions signals you are approaching your limit and it is believed. Concessions of the same size signal there are more available.

**Ask before you argue.** Most positions have a reason behind them that is more flexible than the position. "What's driving the date?" frequently produces an answer that neither side had considered.

**Silence after a proposal is a tool, and it is uncomfortable.** The instinct to fill it by improving your own offer is the most common unforced error in the room.

## Step 4: Negotiate the relationship, not just the deal

- **Separate the person from the problem.** Attack the gap, not the counterpart. In a long relationship this is not softness; it is the only sustainable posture.
- **Give them something they can defend.** Everyone reports to someone. An agreement the other person cannot justify internally will be reopened, whatever they signed.
- **A deal the other side cannot perform is not a win.** A supplier squeezed below cost will underperform, and you will own the consequence. `teaming-and-subcontracts` makes the same point about a team assembled to win rather than to perform.
- **Say what you cannot do, plainly and early.** Ambiguity read as flexibility becomes a bigger problem than a clear no.

## Step 5: Negotiating from a weak position

It happens — you need the work, the alternative is bad, or you are late and asking for relief.

- **Do not pretend.** Bluffing a walk-away you do not have works until it is tested, and it is usually tested.
- **Change the problem instead.** Extend the scope so there is more to trade, bring in a third party, split the decision into stages, or offer something non-monetary that costs you little.
- **Where you have caused the problem**, lead with the diagnosis and the plan rather than the ask — see `program-recovery`. A recovery presented as a request for relief lands worse than the same thing presented as a plan that needs one decision.
- **Accepting a bad deal knowingly is sometimes right.** Doing it while telling yourself it is a good one is how it recurs.

## Step 6: Close it properly

- **Write down what was agreed, the same day**, and send it. Memories diverge within a week and the divergence is never random — each side remembers the version favourable to them.
- **Confirm ambiguities explicitly** rather than leaving them for goodwill. An ambiguity in a negotiation is an ambiguity in a contract, and `sow-and-pws` describes how that resolves later: in favor of whoever wrote it more carefully.
- **Make sure it reaches the people who execute it.** An agreement the delivery team never hears about is not an agreement.
- **Record what you learned about how the other side negotiates.** You will do this again with them — that is `lessons-learned` applied to a relationship.

## Common failures

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positions traded, interests unexplored | Deadlock over a number | Ask what is driving it |
| Walk-away decided in the room | Worse deal, taken under pressure | Decide it in advance, in writing |
| Single-issue sequencing | Value destroyed, every issue a fight | Bundle and trade across |
| Unilateral concessions | Pressure escalates | Never give without getting |
| Negotiating with the wrong person | Position disclosed, nothing gained | Establish authority first |
| Winning against a partner | Poor performance you then own | A deal they cannot perform is not a win |
| Bluffing a weak position | Called, and credibility spent | Change the problem instead |
| Nothing written down | Two versions of the agreement | Same-day confirmation, sent |

The honest one is the fourth from the top, and it applies most inside your own organization: the colleague you concede to without a trade will ask again, and they will be right to.
