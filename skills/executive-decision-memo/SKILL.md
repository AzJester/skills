# Executive decision memo

A decision memo exists to get one specific decision made by one specific person or group. Everything in it either serves that or should be cut.

The instrument matters. A deck is for presenting with you in the room, controlling sequence and answering as you go. A memo is read without you, in five minutes, often twenty minutes before the meeting. If you will not be there, or the decision needs thinking about before the meeting, the memo is the right instrument and a deck is not.

## The shape

One page. Two if the analysis genuinely requires it, and the second page is appendix rather than more argument.

**1. The ask** — one sentence, first line, no preamble.

> *Requesting approval to award the sensor integration subcontract to Vendor B at $2.4M, $310K above the lowest bid.*

Not "This memo discusses the sensor subcontract decision." The reader must know within five seconds what they are being asked to do.

**2. Why now** — one or two sentences. What forces a decision, and what happens if it is deferred. A memo with no deadline gets no decision.

**3. Background** — the minimum to make the decision comprehensible. Three or four sentences. The most common failure in the whole document is background written for someone who knows nothing, sent to someone who knows most of it.

**4. Options** — usually two or three, each with what it costs, what it gets, and what it risks. Include doing nothing where it is genuinely available, because executives will ask.

A table is almost always right here:

| Option | Cost | Schedule | Risk | Effect on the outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

**5. Recommendation** — which one, and the reason in one or two sentences. Recommend. A memo presenting three options with no recommendation transfers work upward, and the person receiving it has less context than you.

**6. What happens next** — who does what, by when, if approved.

## What executives actually check

Having read the ask and the recommendation, they look for:

- **What does this cost, in total, including what I cannot see?** Second-order costs, sustainment, opportunity cost of the people involved.
- **What happens if we do nothing?** If the answer is "nothing much", the memo may not need to exist.
- **What is the risk, and what is being done about it?** A memo with no risks reads as one that has not been examined. Name the real one — `risk-management` for the framing.
- **Who disagrees, and why?** Dissent surfaced in the memo is a credibility asset. Dissent surfaced in the meeting by someone else is not.
- **What is the decision I am actually making?** Approving a subcontract is different from approving a vendor relationship, which is different from approving an architecture. Be precise about the scope of the decision.

## Writing it

**Lead with the answer.** The Pyramid Principle applies — your account's `mck-pyramid-checker` covers this. Conclusion first, support after. A memo that builds to its point in the last paragraph will be read in the wrong order or not at all.

**Numbers with sources.** Every figure that matters carries where it came from — an estimate, a quote, an actual. An unattributed number is challenged first and undermines the rest.

**Distinguish what you know from what you assume.** State assumptions as assumptions. An executive who discovers an unmarked assumption stops trusting the unmarked facts too.

**Cut the hedging.** "It may be advisable to consider" is a sentence with no content. If you are uncertain, say what would resolve the uncertainty and what it would cost to find out.

**Write for the person, not the role.** What do they already know, what do they care about, what have they been burned by? A memo to someone who has been through a failed integration should address transition risk whether or not it is the largest risk.

## Length discipline

If it will not fit on a page, one of these is true and worth diagnosing rather than accepting:

- The background is written for the wrong reader.
- There are too many options — narrow before writing, not in the memo.
- The analysis belongs in an appendix or a linked artefact.
- The decision is actually several decisions, and should be several memos or one sequenced memo.

## When not to write one

- The decision is already made and you want cover. That is a notification.
- You need discussion rather than a decision. Convene the discussion.
- The decision is genuinely theirs to originate rather than to approve. Bring the problem, not a recommendation.
- The information is a status update. A status update in decision-memo form wastes the reader's attention on looking for an ask that is not there.
