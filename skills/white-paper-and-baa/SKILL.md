# White papers, BAAs and SBIR

The path most defense research and prototyping work actually starts on. A BAA, CSO or SBIR topic is not an RFP, and writing one like an RFP response is the most common way to lose before the technical content is ever read.

**The core difference: an RFP response demonstrates compliance; a white paper sells an idea.** The RFP tells you the requirement and scores your conformance to it. A BAA states a broad interest and asks you to propose the problem *and* the solution. The reader is usually a technical program manager with discretion, not a source-selection board with a rubric.

## Step 1: Know which instrument you are answering

They are not interchangeable, and the differences change what you write.

| Instrument | Shape | What wins |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **BAA** | Standing or annual announcement of broad research interest; usually two-step, white paper then invited full proposal | Scientific or technical merit, and fit to the sponsor's actual program |
| **CSO** | Solicits innovative commercial solutions; faster, more flexible award instruments | Maturity, commercial viability, and speed to demonstrable capability |
| **SBIR / STTR** | Topic-driven, phased, small-business set-aside; STTR requires a research-institution partner | Answering the specific topic, feasibility in Phase I, transition path throughout |
| **Unsolicited white paper** | You initiate, outside any announcement | A problem the sponsor already has and has not solved, plus a reason it is you |
| **RFP** | Requirement stated, compliance scored | See `proposal-writing` — a different discipline |

**Read the announcement's own instructions before anything else.** Page limits, required sections, formatting and submission mechanics vary by announcement and by amendment, and they are enforced. Where this skill and the announcement disagree, the announcement wins. Figures, ceilings and durations change year to year — take them from the current solicitation, never from memory.

## Step 2: Understand what a white paper is for

**Its job is to earn an invitation, not to win the award.** A white paper that tries to be a full proposal in ten pages fails at both. The reader is deciding one thing: is this worth asking for a full proposal about.

That reframes every section. Enough detail to make the idea credible and distinguishable; not enough to answer questions nobody has asked yet. Depth that belongs in the full proposal spent early is depth the reader has to wade through to reach the idea.

## Step 3: The structure that works

Most white papers, whatever the announcement calls the sections, need these five things in this order:

**1. The problem, in the sponsor's terms.** Not your capability. The operational or technical gap, stated so the program manager recognizes their own problem. If you cannot state the problem in the sponsor's language, you do not yet understand the opportunity well enough to bid it.

**2. The innovation, stated as a claim.** One or two sentences naming what is new. "We apply machine learning to sensor data" is not a claim — everyone says it. "We show that a 12-megabyte model running on the existing radio's DSP recovers 90% of the classification accuracy of the current cloud pipeline, with no reachback" is a claim: specific, falsifiable, and it says why this matters.

**3. The technical approach, at the level of how it could fail.** Enough for a technical reader to judge whether it is plausible. Name the hard part. A white paper with no visible hard part reads either as trivial or as not yet understood, and both lose to one that names its risk and says how Phase I retires it.

**4. Objectives and what "done" looks like.** Concrete, measurable deliverables, with the metric that decides success stated before the work starts. Vague objectives are the single most common technical-reviewer complaint.

**5. Why you, and where it goes next.** Relevant capability, the team, and the transition path. For SBIR especially, transition is scored throughout, not only in Phase III.

Rough cost and period of performance go where the announcement asks for them, at the fidelity it asks for — usually a range, not a built-up estimate.

## Step 4: Write for the technical reviewer

The reviewer is a subject-matter expert reading many submissions, often quickly, and usually looking for a reason to stop reading.

- **Lead with the idea.** Not with company background. The first paragraph either contains the innovation or the reviewer is already skimming.
- **Claims that can be checked.** Prior results, a working prototype, published work, a measured number. `engineering-to-proposal` harvests this from real delivered work.
- **Name the risk yourself.** A reviewer who spots an unacknowledged risk stops trusting the rest. One that is named and mitigated reads as competence.
- **Cite honestly, and know the state of the art.** A proposal that is unaware of published work in its own area fails technical merit regardless of the idea.
- **One figure that carries the concept.** Frequently the only part read twice — see `architecture-diagrams`.
- **Distinguish what exists from what is proposed.** Blurring current capability into proposed work is the fastest way to lose a technical reviewer's trust, and it is usually detectable.

## Step 5: SBIR and STTR specifics

**Phase structure**, with the amounts and durations set by the current solicitation:

| Phase | Purpose | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Feasibility — establish technical merit and the path forward | Proposing to build the product instead of proving the risky part |
| II | Prototype development from Phase I results | Treating it as more Phase I rather than as a demonstrable capability |
| III | Commercialisation and transition; no SBIR funds, but sole-source authority follows from prior phases | Not building the customer relationship during I and II, so there is nobody to transition to |

Some agencies offer a Direct-to-Phase-II route where feasibility can already be shown. Availability and rules vary by agency and cycle.

Four things that decide SBIR outcomes more than technical quality:

- **Answer the topic that was written, not the topic you wish had been.** Topic authors evaluate against their own topic. A strong proposal against an adjacent problem loses to an adequate one on target.
- **Eligibility is checked and is fatal.** Small-business status, ownership, place of performance, principal-investigator employment, and for STTR the research-institution partnership and the work-split percentages. Confirm against the current solicitation before writing.
- **Commercialisation is scored in Phase I.** Not deferred to Phase III. A named transition customer beats a market-size paragraph.
- **The proposal is a contract if you win.** What is promised in the work plan is what gets audited. Do not over-promise into a Phase I schedule.

## Common failures

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capability statement in disguise | Opens with company history | Open with the sponsor's problem |
| No falsifiable claim | "Leverages AI/ML to enhance…" | State a specific, checkable result |
| No visible hard part | Reads as already solved | Name the risk; say how the phase retires it |
| Full proposal in ten pages | Depth nobody asked for yet | Earn the invitation; save depth for the proposal |
| Ignoring the topic's words | Adjacent problem, well argued | Answer the topic as written |
| Transition as an afterthought | Strong tech, no customer | Name the transition path from Phase I |
| Announcement not re-read | Format or eligibility non-compliance | The announcement governs; check the amendments |

The honest one: most rejected white papers are not rejected for being wrong. They are rejected for not making it obvious, in the first half page, what is new and why this sponsor should care.
