# Chicago and Turabian

Chicago is two systems, not one. Choosing between them is the first decision and the one most often got wrong — a document mixing them signals that nobody established which was in use.

**Turabian** is the student-oriented adaptation of Chicago. Same systems, adjusted for coursework and theses, with formatting requirements a publisher would otherwise set. Where a university specifies Turabian, its requirements sit on top of Chicago's.

## Which system

| | Notes–bibliography | Author–date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Citation appears as | A superscript number, with a footnote or endnote | `(Author 2024, 45)` in the text |
| Used in | History, literature, the arts, policy writing | Sciences, social sciences |
| End list | Bibliography | References |
| Strength | Discursive notes; accommodates archival and unusual sources | Compact; author and date immediately visible |

Notes–bibliography is what most people mean by "Chicago style" and is the one that handles the sources humanities and policy work actually cites — archives, government documents, interviews, manuscripts. Author–date resembles APA in shape but differs in detail, and the resemblance is a trap: the punctuation and capitalisation are not the same.

**Pick one, state it, and do not mix.**

## Notes–bibliography

A superscript number in the text points to a note. Notes may sit at the foot of the page or collected as endnotes; footnotes are more usual in books and theses.

**First full note:**
```
1. Firstname Lastname, Title of Book in Italics (City: Publisher, 2023), 45.
```

**Shortened note** for every subsequent citation of that work:
```
2. Lastname, Short Title, 78.
```

The shortened form is standard from the second citation onward. `Ibid.` has fallen out of favor in current Chicago and the shortened note is preferred — a document using `Ibid.` throughout reads as following an older edition.

**Bibliography entry** — note the differences from the note form, which is where errors concentrate:
```
Lastname, Firstname. Title of Book in Italics. City: Publisher, 2023.
```

Three differences to hold: the author is inverted, the elements are separated by periods rather than commas, and there are no parentheses around publication details.

**Common types**

```
Journal article, note
1. Firstname Lastname, "Title of Article," Journal Name 12, no. 3 (2024): 123.

Journal article, bibliography
Lastname, Firstname. "Title of Article." Journal Name 12, no. 3 (2024): 120–45.

Chapter, note
1. Firstname Lastname, "Chapter Title," in Book Title, ed. Firstname Lastname
   (City: Publisher, 2023), 100.

Government document, bibliography
U.S. Department of Defense. Title of Document. Washington, DC: Government
   Publishing Office, 2024.

Archival material, note
1. Lastname to Lastname, March 15, 1944, box 12, folder 3, Collection Name,
   Repository, City.

Website, note
1. "Page Title," Organization, accessed March 15, 2024, https://url.
```

Archival citation is where notes–bibliography earns its place. The note form accommodates a description of the item, its container, and its repository in a way author–date systems cannot.

## Author–date

```
In text:      (Lastname 2024, 45)
              Lastname (2024, 45) argues that...

References:   Lastname, Firstname. 2024. Title of Book in Italics.
                  City: Publisher.

              Lastname, Firstname. 2024. "Title of Article." Journal Name
                  12 (3): 120–45.
```

Note against APA, since the two look similar and are not: the year follows the author without parentheses in the reference list, journal issue is in parentheses after the volume, and page ranges are not preceded by `pp.`

## Formatting

- **Title case for all titles** — books, articles, chapters. This differs from both APA and IEEE, which use sentence case for article titles.
- Book and journal titles in italics; article and chapter titles in quotation marks.
- Page ranges condensed per Chicago's rules — `120–45`, not `120–145`. Exceptions: numbers under 100 and multiples of 100 keep all digits (`96–117`, `100–104`); for x01–x09 use only the changed part (`101–8`); otherwise use two digits, more where needed.
- Notes numbered consecutively through a chapter or article.
- Bibliography alphabetical by author surname, hanging indent.
- Three or more authors: in notes, first author plus `et al.`; in the bibliography, all listed up to six, and for seven or more the first three plus `et al.` (Chicago 18th. Under the 17th and Turabian 9, notes use `et al.` only at four or more authors, and the bibliography lists up to ten.)

**Turabian additions** where a university specifies it: double-spaced text with single-spaced block quotes and notes, one-inch margins, page numbers in a defined position, and a title page rather than a heading block. The university's own thesis requirements sit above Turabian and win where they differ.

## Choosing between the two, in practice

If the venue specifies, follow it. Otherwise: if you cite archives, government documents, interviews or manuscripts, or if your notes carry commentary as well as citations, use notes–bibliography. If your sources are mostly journal articles and books and you want the date visible in the text, use author–date.

For defense policy writing, notes–bibliography is usually the better fit — it handles government documents, unpublished reports and interviews cleanly, and a note can carry the classification or distribution caveat on a source.

## Reference

Consult the current Chicago Manual of Style, and the venue's or university's own requirements, which override.
